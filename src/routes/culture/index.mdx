---
title: Culture & Sumud
description: The idea of sumud, and the land, food, craft, language and literature through which Palestinians have kept a national identity alive across a century of loss.
---

Sumud (صمود) means steadfastness, or perseverance -- literally, to stand firm. It entered Palestinian political vocabulary in earnest after 1967, when the term came to describe something quite specific: the decision to stay. To remain on the land, in the village, in the refugee camp, in the face of occupation, dispossession or exile, rather than leave. It is not passive endurance. Sumud has always carried an active, almost stubborn charge -- planting a tree you may never see mature, rebuilding a home you know may be demolished again, sending your children to school under curfew. Staying, as a form of resistance in itself.

That idea runs through everything else on this page, because Palestinian culture since 1948 has had to do double duty: it is simply how people live, and it is also, unavoidably, an act of continuity against a history that has tried repeatedly to sever it.

## The olive tree

No single image carries more weight in Palestinian culture than the olive tree. Groves across the West Bank hold trees that are centuries, in some cases over a thousand years, old -- living property passed down through families for generations, tied to specific plots of land in a way that predates modern land registries entirely. The autumn olive harvest is a communal event: extended families, often including relatives who travel back from the diaspora specifically for it, spend weeks picking together.

That same tree has become a site of conflict. Human rights organisations have documented the uprooting of tens of thousands of olive trees over the decades, through settlement expansion, the route of the separation barrier, military land clearance and, increasingly, settler violence timed to coincide with the harvest itself. A tree that takes decades to mature and can bear fruit for centuries is destroyed in minutes. Palestinians and Israeli solidarity volunteers alike now organise seasonal efforts to protect harvests in vulnerable areas -- one of the more literal expressions of sumud there is.

## Tatreez: embroidery as memory

Palestinian cross-stitch embroidery, tatreez, is one of the most distinctive Palestinian art forms, traditionally worked into the thobe, a long dress whose colours, motifs and stitch patterns once varied so precisely by village and region that an observer could tell where a woman was from by looking at her dress. Motifs carry names drawn from the natural and domestic world around them -- cypress trees, moons, feathers -- and were passed from mother to daughter as both craft and record.

After 1948, tatreez took on a new function. Refugee women, separated from their home villages, began combining motifs from different regions into single garments -- sometimes deliberately, as a way of holding multiple lost places within one piece of cloth. Tatreez patterns have since become widely recognised symbols of Palestinian identity, appearing on everything from wedding dresses to protest banners, and UNESCO inscribed the craft on its Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity in 2021.

## Visual art and protest symbols

Naji al-Ali, born in 1938 in the Galilee village of al-Shajara (destroyed in 1948) and raised in the Ain al-Hilweh refugee camp in Lebanon, created the cartoon figure Handala in 1969: a barefoot ten-year-old refugee boy, always drawn from behind, hands clasped at his back, who appeared in nearly every one of al-Ali's newspaper cartoons for the rest of his life. Al-Ali said Handala would only turn to face the reader once Palestinians could return to their homes; the boy has stayed ten years old, and turned away, ever since. Al-Ali was shot outside a London newspaper's offices in 1987 and died of his injuries weeks later; no investigation has conclusively established who was responsible. Handala's silhouette is now reproduced worldwide, on keychains, tattoos and painted walls, as a shorthand for Palestinian dispossession that needs no caption.

The keffiyeh, a black-and-white scarf in a herringbone or fishnet weave, has a similar arc: rural, working dress that became a national symbol during the 1936-39 Arab revolt against British rule, and an internationally recognised one once it was permanently associated with Yasser Arafat's image from the late 1960s onward. Nearly all keffiyehs sold today are imported cheaply from factories in China; the Hirbawi factory in Hebron, founded in 1961, is the last one still weaving them in Palestine, and buying from it has become its own small act of sumud. Worn at protests far beyond the region, the keffiyeh carries enough weight as a political symbol that some schools, workplaces and public institutions have moved to ban it outright, which has, if anything, only sharpened what it signals.

## Food and hospitality

Palestinian cuisine shares much with the wider Levant, but carries its own dishes and its own disputes: musakhan, roasted chicken over taboon bread with caramelised onions, sumac and pine nuts, is widely regarded as Palestine's national dish. Maqluba ("upside-down"), a layered rice, meat and vegetable dish flipped from its pot at the table, and knafeh, the syrup-soaked cheese pastry associated above all with the city of Nablus, are both sources of considerable regional pride. Food, like embroidery, has become part of a wider argument about cultural attribution across the region -- an argument that matters to Palestinians less as a culinary technicality than as one more instance of a broader, lived experience: watching things that are self-evidently theirs be described, in the world's eyes, as belonging to someone else, or to no one in particular.

Hospitality itself carries weight. Guests are served first and served generously, coffee is poured and refused only after a ritual third cup, and the obligation to host well is taken seriously even, and especially, in the refugee camps and under blockade, where doing so is hardest.

## Language, poetry and Mahmoud Darwish

Palestinian Arabic carries its own dialect features and, especially since 1948, its own vocabulary of loss and return: al-Nakba, al-awda (the return), al-shatat (the diaspora), muhajir and laji' for the different legal statuses of the displaced. Literature has done enormous work in this culture precisely because so much else -- land, archives, institutions -- was taken or destroyed.

Mahmoud Darwish (1941-2008), born in the Galilee village of al-Birwa which was depopulated and destroyed in 1948, is the poet most closely associated with the Palestinian national experience, though he resisted being reduced to a political symbol and insisted, across a long career, on poetry that was also simply about love, exile, language and mortality. Ghassan Kanafani, killed by a car bomb in Beirut in 1972, wrote some of the defining fiction of the refugee experience, including the novella Men in the Sun. Fadwa Tuqan, Edward Said, Susan Abulhawa and many others have carried that literary tradition forward in different forms and different languages, for readers inside Palestine and across a diaspora spread over every continent.

## Film

Palestinian cinema has no national film industry or state funding body behind it, so most of its best-known work has been made through international co-production, and often by directors working in exile or the diaspora. Elia Suleiman, born in Nazareth, built a distinctive body of work -- Divine Intervention (2002), which won the Jury Prize at Cannes, and It Must Be Heaven (2019) -- out of near-wordless, deadpan scenes of daily life under occupation, closer in spirit to silent comedy than to conventional political cinema. 5 Broken Cameras (2011), co-directed by the Palestinian farmer Emad Burnat and the Israeli filmmaker Guy Davidi from years of footage Burnat shot of his village Bil'in's protests against the separation barrier, was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Documentary Feature in 2013. Farha (2021), a Jordanian production following a teenage girl through the Nakba, drew condemnation from Israeli government ministers when Netflix added it in 2022, who called for its removal -- itself a reminder of how contested even a single film about 1948 remains more than seventy-five years on.

## Dabke and public celebration

Dabke, a line dance performed at weddings and celebrations across the Levant, has a specifically Palestinian repertoire of steps and has, like tatreez, become something performed deliberately at diaspora gatherings and cultural festivals as an assertion of continuity -- proof, staged in a hall in Amman or Santiago or Detroit, that a culture forcibly scattered has not stopped dancing.

None of this exists to illustrate a history lesson. It is how a people who have spent most of a century being told, in various ways, that they do not exist as a people, have gone on living as one -- which is, in the end, what sumud has always meant.
